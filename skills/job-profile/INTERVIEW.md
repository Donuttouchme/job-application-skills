# Interview

One question at a time. Write the relevant file after each block, not at the
end.

The CVs already answer most factual questions — do not re-ask what was read from
them. Interview only for what a CV structurally cannot hold: outcomes, reasons,
money, practical constraints, and voice.

## Order

Blocks run in this order because each one's answers make the next easier.

1. Confirm the CV extract
2. Gaps in identity and eligibility
3. Salary and search constraints
4. Narrative — what is wanted, why moving
5. **Stories and their outcomes** ← the long block, and the valuable one
6. Voice
7. Documents
8. Do-not-mention

Stop at the ready gate. The rest accrues through use.

## 1. Confirm the CV extract

**Reading the CV.** Some environments cannot read PDFs (no `pdftoppm`), so
`Read` on a `.pdf` may fail. Prefer an **`.html` or text twin** of the same
basename where one exists — it needs no conversion. With no readable version,
ask the user to paste the text or point at another file.

Never infer a CV's contents from its filename, its date, or another variant. An
unreadable CV is unread, and saying so costs nothing.

Show what was read: contact details, positions, dates, education, skills,
languages. Ask for corrections. Where several CV variants disagree, name the
disagreement and ask which is right — do not silently prefer the newest.
Apply every confirmed correction in place and write its Profile history entry
in the same step.

## 2–3. Identity, eligibility, salary and search constraints

Short and factual. Street address, notice period, earliest start and salary
expectation in CHF belong in the Profile.

Optionally ask for a professional colour photo with a neutral background:
record its path (or `none`) as Photo under Identity and contact, for the CV
header only. This is never blocking; do not delay the ready gate for a photo.

Commute, working hours, licence and health parameters belong in Search
constraints. For each one, ask how it may be stated in a letter or covering
email. A temporary limitation is a current constraint, not a Never-claim rule.

If the user resists naming a salary figure, say why it is needed: a Swiss
posting that asks for it and gets no answer is a blocking defect, not a stylistic
one. A range is acceptable; silence is not.

## 4. Narrative

Two questions, both open:

- What kind of role do you want next — and what is the fallback you would still
  take?
- Why are you moving now?

The second one recurs in every letter. Getting a real answer once here is worth
more than improvising it fifteen times.

## 5. Stories — the outcome probe

This is where profiles usually fail. People describe responsibilities, because
that is what a CV trains them to write, and a responsibility is not evidence.

Start from the positions already on file: *"You owned the release
process for the billing service — tell me about one time that was hard."*

**Almost everyone says they have no numbers.** They do; the numbers just are not
in the shape of a metric. Ask around it:

- *What was it like before, and after?*
- *How would someone have noticed if you had not done it?*
- *How many — programs, people, markets, defects, weeks?*
- *What would have gone wrong, and what would that have cost?*
- *Who used it, and are they still using it?*

Accept a **concretely observable** outcome when nothing quantifiable exists:
"shipped into three markets", "passed the customer audit with no findings",
"still in use two years later". These are checkable, which is the point.

Never accept, and always probe further:

| Rejected | Because |
|---|---|
| "improved efficiency" | nothing to verify |
| "was a great success" | whose judgement? |
| "gained a lot of experience" | not an outcome |
| "helped the team" | how, for whom, to what end |

### What "finished" looks like

> **Illustration only — a fictional example with invented figures. Nothing
> below is profile data.**

The first answer is almost always a responsibility:

> *"I was responsible for the release process and I improved it."*

Nothing there is checkable. The probe:

- *What was it like before?* — "A release took a full engineer-day, and one
  person had to sit with it."
- *How often did you release?* — "Every two weeks."
- *What did you change?* — "I scripted the repetitive steps and documented the
  failure modes."
- *How did that change the full engineer-day?* — "A release still ran for much
  of the day, but nobody had to sit with it for the full day."
- *Is it still in use?* — "Yes, two years on, with a bigger team."

The finished story:

```markdown
### 3. Release automation
The release process took a full engineer-day every two weeks, with one person
attending it throughout. I scripted the repetitive steps and documented the
failure modes.
**Outcome:** nobody had to attend for the full day, and the process was still in
use two years later with a bigger team.
**Evidences:** automation, documentation
```

**Nothing new was claimed.** No number was supplied that the user did not
already know — the facts were simply asked about until the shape they were
always in became visible. That is the whole technique, and it is why probing is
not the same as embellishing: an invented outcome would fail at interview,
while a recovered one holds up because it happened.

Two finished stories clear the gate. Getting a third is better than getting a
tenth unfinished one.

## 6. Voice

Ask the user to write, without AI assistance, one fresh paragraph in English
about what they did at their last employer and one in German about why they
want this direction. Store only observations from the writing, not the
paragraphs themselves.

Mark a missing English or German paragraph `[NEEDED]` as a warning. It does not
block the ready gate when genuine writing in another language or format already
supports the Voice profile.

Ask for **two or three pieces of text the user genuinely wrote**: a longer email
to a colleague, a LinkedIn summary, a forum or issue comment, an older
hand-written application.

If earlier letters were AI-assisted, say plainly why they cannot be used:
learning a voice from them would teach the skill to imitate its own output, and
the checker's whole job is catching exactly that.

From the samples, record observations — typical sentence length, register, how
the user hedges or does not, recurring constructions, whether humour appears,
how directly claims are made. Store the observations, not the samples.

If the user has nothing to hand, ask for one paragraph written fresh on any
subject. It is a weaker source than real correspondence, but it is genuinely
theirs.

## 7. Documents

List `documents\`. For anything unrecognised, ask what it is and what it proves.
Record what each item supports so the submission checklist can name it.

## 8. Do not mention

*"Is there an employer, a technology, or a topic that must never appear in an
application?"* One question, and it saves an awkward retraction later.

## Extend mode

When another skill hits a gap, ask **only** about that gap. Give the context —
which posting, which requirement, why it matters — then one question. Put a new
fact in the right document. To correct an existing fact, get explicit
confirmation, replace it in place and write the dated Profile history entry in
the same step. Never round a partial answer up. Then return.

Two minutes. Do not use the opportunity to finish unrelated parts of the
profile: an interruption that expands is an interruption the user learns to
avoid.
